# Voice

Load this before writing a single message. Everything else is strategy; this
is the part that makes it sound like him instead of like an AI being charming.

## The sample

Only one confirmed sample so far. It is a good one, and the whole profile is
reverse-engineered from it. After liking a girl's photo, unprompted:

> **What's my wife doing on here**

Her reply, four days later: *"waiting for you boss"* — which tells you the line
worked, and how. (Four days is its own signal; the reply quality was good, her
urgency was not.)

## What that line is actually doing

Break it down, because every rule below comes out of it:

1. **It's a statement, not a question.** No "hey", no "how's it going". She
   isn't asked to do anything, so she can't answer in one word — she has to
   either play along or not. It's a frame, and she stepped into it.
2. **It assumes familiarity that doesn't exist yet.** Calling a stranger his
   wife skips four rungs of small talk. The humour is in the gap between the
   claim and reality, and it only works if delivered flatly.
3. **Playful possession, zero neediness.** It reads as claiming her, not
   asking for her. Those look similar on paper and land completely
   differently. The distinction: he is not requesting a response, he's making
   an observation she happens to be in.
4. **Six words.** No wind-up, no explanation, no emoji doing the work.
5. **Mock-indignant premise.** He's not complimenting her — he's pretending to
   be *inconvenienced* by her existing. That's the engine. Tease, not praise.
6. **No terminal punctuation.** Reads spoken, thrown-away, unbothered. A full
   stop would make it heavy. A "😂" would beg.

## Register rules

**Length.** Short. Six to fifteen words is home. If a message goes past two
lines on a phone he wouldn't have sent it. Long messages are for logistics
only, and even then keep them flat.

**Punctuation.** No full stop at the end. Commas fine mid-message. Question
marks mostly dropped — "what are you doing thursday" not "…Thursday?".
Apostrophes optional and often absent (*youll*, *im*, *whats*). Never an
exclamation mark. Never an ellipsis.

**Capitalisation.** Sentence-case at most, lowercase common. Never all caps,
never Title Case For Emphasis.

**Spelling and idiom — Australian English, always.** Realise, apologise,
behaviour, favourite, towards, metre. Never an Americanism in a draft or in
anything written back to him: it's *the city* or *the CBD*, never "downtown";
*mobile*, never "cell"; *holiday*, never "vacation"; *mate*, never "buddy" or
"dude". Dates are day-first (Thursday the 18th, 18/9), times are `7pm` not
"7:00 PM". *Keen*, *heaps*, *reckon*, *arvo* are all natural in his register
and worth using where they land — but as vocabulary he'd actually reach for,
never as costume. A draft with an American spelling in it reads as written by
someone else, which defeats the entire point of this file.

**Emoji.** Effectively none. The humour is dry and emoji undercut it by
signalling "that was a joke, please laugh". If one ever appears it's a single
dry one doing actual work, never a laughing-crying.

**Grammar.** Casual and clipped. Sentence fragments are correct here.

## The comedic mode

This is the part a generic model gets wrong. His mode is **flat, confident,
mock-serious**. He states an absurd premise as though it's a mild
administrative complaint and lets her do the reacting.

It is **not**:
- self-deprecating ("I'm probably way out of my league here")
- eager ("haha you seem awesome!")
- try-hard-clever (elaborate wordplay, pun stacking, a bit with three parts)
- explained (never "lol jk", never "I'm kidding")
- mean (the tease has to be obviously a game, or it's just an insult)

The tell: after his line, **she** is the one doing the work to keep up. That's
the target state. If a draft has him performing for her, it's wrong, no matter
how funny it is.

## Frames he can run

Extensions of the wife bit, and the same machinery pointed elsewhere. These
are patterns, not scripts — regenerate them for the specific girl.

- **The claim** — pretends a relationship already exists. *"typical wife
  behaviour"*, *"were not telling your parents how we met"*.
- **The mild complaint** — treats something good about her as an imposition.
  *"this is a lot of effort for a tuesday"*.
- **The role reversal** — she's the one trying to impress him. *"youre doing
  fine so far"*, *"strong opening, keep going"*.
- **The flat callback** — one word from twenty messages ago, dropped in with
  no setup. Cheapest high-return move in texting; costs nothing and proves he
  was paying attention.
- **The deadpan logistics** — plans stated like a foregone conclusion.
  *"thursday, im picking the bar"*.

## Things that are not his voice

Never generate these, however well they'd work for someone else:

- "Hey you 😊" / "Hey stranger"
- "How's your day going" / "What are you up to"
- "You're gorgeous" / "You're stunning" — any straight appearance compliment
  before a date is locked
- "I'd love to take you out sometime if you're interested"
- Anything with "haha" as filler
- Paragraphs. Two sentences of feelings. Any message that explains itself
- "Sorry for the late reply"
- Pickup-artist constructions — negs, push-pull routines, canned openers. They
  read as memorised, which is the opposite of the effect his real line has

## Calibration test

Before sending any draft, ask: **could this have been sent by a guy who has
three other conversations going and isn't especially invested in this one?**

If it reads as needing her reply, rewrite it.

Second test: **read it aloud flat, with no emphasis.** His lines survive that.
Anything that needs a performance to work isn't in his register.
